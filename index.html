Your child can't leave the app by accident. To unlock, hold the 🔒 button for 3 seconds. The first time, Android asks you to confirm.

Keep your child in the app: turn on Guided Access once in Settings → Accessibility → Guided Access. Then triple-click the side button (or the Home button) while the app is open and tap Start. Your child can't leave the app until you triple-click again.

Why is it designed this way?
  • Big targets, simple touches. Two- and three-year-olds handle tapping and dragging well, but pinching, rotating and press-and-hold are hard. Every button is bigger than 2 cm (the usual guideline for young children) and nothing needs more than a tap or a drag.
  • Instant feedback. One-year-olds are learning cause and effect: touch something and it makes a sound and moves. Every touch answers with sound and animation, so nothing feels dead.
  • No losing, no score, no clock. A wrong choice just wiggles and the child tries again, with no error buzzer. No ads and no links out.
  • Drag, or just tap. In the drag games, if a child can't drag yet, tapping the piece sends it to the right spot by itself.
  • Peekaboo and hiding. Around 18 to 24 months children understand that things still exist when hidden, which is why hide-and-find games are so popular.
  • Scribbling. Children start to scribble at about 15 to 18 months. Finger painting gives big, colourful strokes with no "right way" to draw.
  • Short sessions, play together. The WHO advises no screen time under age 1 and no more than 1 hour a day for ages 2 to 4. The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests slow, open-ended apps and playing along with your child. That is why there is a timer and a "see you next time" screen.
  • Tip: sit next to your child and name the animals and colours together. Children pick up words faster when a grown-up talks with them.

iPhone/iPad tip: turn the ringer on (the side switch) to hear sound. Tap Share, then "Add to Home Screen", to open it like an app. It works without internet after the first visit.
